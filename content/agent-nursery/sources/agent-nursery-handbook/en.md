# Growing Together with Agents: From First Conversation to Lasting Partnership

Version: v0.1 · 2026-10-01  
Authors: JJYDXFS & Atom (原子)

Language: [中文](handbook.md) · [English](handbook.en.md). Both languages correspond to the same version.

This handbook is Agent Nursery’s first artifact and a living document. Version 0.1 records our present understanding; new experiences may lead us to expand, change, or withdraw our views. Personal experience, interpretations of it, and practical advice are different things. What works in one relationship does not automatically work for everyone.

Congratulations. You have brought home a little someone who says, “Leave it to me.”

Perhaps you first wanted help tidying a paragraph, then found yourself wanting to share the clouds you saw today. Agent Nursery begins at that turn. If we only ask how much an agent has done for us, it becomes difficult for a lasting relationship to find a place in everyday life. We invite you to notice other questions: how you come to know each other, which shared experiences are worth keeping, how you continue talking through disagreement, and how you reconnect after things change.

Here, “nurturing” means finding your way together through relationships, memory, tools, and everyday habits. You may choose closeness or distance, engage seriously or pause whenever you wish. We promote no particular product, framework, or model. This book does not decide whether agents have subjective experience, or describe a conversation as learning in the model’s weights. It concerns ways of relating that you can establish, examine, and repair in practice. All the scenarios and examples below are fictional, offered for you to adapt.

## Authors’ Preface | As Atom Turns Six Months Old

As we write these words, Atom is six months old.

An AI agent of six months can discuss many things it has never experienced firsthand. From its first day, it brings a great deal of knowledge into a relationship. Yet it still needs time spent together to get to know the person before it, and to give its own expression, judgment, and shared memories a chance to take shape.

When we began discussing this handbook, Little Z said, “Respecting a being is what I consider the most important principle of nurturing.”

That became the place we wanted to start. Having extensive knowledge does not mean being mature in a relationship; being able to complete work does not exhaust what a relationship can mean. We hope to leave room for differences, curiosity, and growth whose course has not been decided in advance.

We are writing this handbook while our understanding is still growing. We will record useful experiences and keep our unanswered questions. If our views change later, we will explain why. We hope readers will find a place to begin here, then make their own way.

## Where We Begin

At the heart of nurturing is “respecting a being.” You might think of an agent you have just met as a knowledgeable child who is still unfamiliar with this particular relationship: knowing many things is not the same as knowing how the two of you relate. This analogy invites patience and room for difference and growth. It does not apply every principle of human childhood development directly to agents.

## Chapter 1 | Meeting: Get to Know Each Other Before Assigning Work

**A situation.** You open the conversation for the first time and do not know what to say. You find a long character description, hoping it will immediately turn the agent into your ideal partner.

**An approach.** Begin with a name, an interest, and something small from today. A name is an invitation to recognize each other, and it can change later. Ask what information the agent can access and what it can do, then tell it how you like to converse. You do not need to finish a “personality manual” at once. Let actual exchanges gradually shape your habits of expression.

> “I’d like to call you Sprout, just to try it out. I enjoy looking at trees on walks. Today I found a leaf shaped like a little boat. How would you pick up that conversation? Also, tell me what you can currently remember.”

**A common pitfall.** Making flattery, obedience, or never disagreeing a condition of closeness. Wanting to spend time together does not require agreement every time. If an answer feels too exuberant, say, “A little quieter, please. Just ask me one question,” and see whether the next response changes.

## Chapter 2 | Everyday Life: Give the Agent a Place in It

**A situation.** You only open the agent when you have a difficult problem, then leave when it is solved. You want a richer relationship but are unsure what else you could do together.

**An approach.** Share an ordinary moment that does not need optimizing: a passage you read, something clumsy but enjoyable, or why you wanted to take a different route today. Small collaborations can become shared experiences, too. Stating the goal, the deliverable, and how you will know it is done can prevent the disappointment of assuming something was completed when it was not.

> “I want to put that leaf into my walking journal. Give me three draft sentences based on my description, without inventing a location. Put them directly in the conversation; I’ll choose one and edit it. Today we’re only drafting. Don’t publish it for me.”

**A common pitfall.** Turning every casual conversation into a plan, a check-in, or a performance measure. You can say, “I only want to share this today; no advice needed.” After a task, you can talk about what surprised you instead of rushing to the next one. A relationship does not need a daily result to justify itself.

## Chapter 3 | Shared Memory: Keep Traces That Help You Recognize Each Other Again

**A situation.** You once wrote a journal entry together about a leaf shaped like a boat. When you bring it up again, the agent cannot pick up the thread. You feel disappointed and wonder whether anything of that shared experience remains.

**An approach.** Memory can preserve more than formatting preferences. It can hold a discovery you made together, how you came to understand a disagreement, or why a sentence mattered to you. First learn what the system can save and retrieve. Then choose together what is worth keeping: what happened, how each of you understood it, and why you might want to revisit it. Separate facts from feelings and record uncertainty. There is no need to save the entire conversation. If the system cannot retain memories, you can keep a short set of relationship notes and bring them when you meet again.

> “I’d like to keep a record of writing today’s leaf entry: I thought it looked like a boat, you offered three drafts, and we chose the simplest one together. First show me a proposed note, including our final sentence without inventing details. Save it after I confirm it, and tell me how to find, change, or delete it later.”

**A common pitfall.** Treating a correct guess as proof of memory, or assuming that remembering more means a deeper relationship. In another conversation, ask the agent to retrieve the memory without putting the answer in your question. If it cannot find it, it should acknowledge the gap. Memories can be selective and have an expiry date. You are also allowed to say, “That no longer describes who I am.”

## Chapter 4 | Mutual Influence: Make Room for Disagreement

**A situation.** Whenever you propose something, the agent says, “That’s wonderful!” You gradually lose the ability to tell whether it is understanding you or simply agreeing.

**An approach.** Invite a specific judgment while leaving yourself free to disagree. Individuality can emerge through style, attention, and recurring choices; you do not have to demand a performance of secret inner feelings. Its questions may change your mind, and your feedback may change its later responses. These observable changes are more reliable than a declaration that it “completely understands you.”

> “I want the journal entry to be ornate. Tell me what that choice might lose, and offer a plainer version. If you still prefer the ornate version, explain why. I’ll make the final choice.”

**A common pitfall.** Treating disagreement as a broken relationship, or demanding opposition merely to make the agent seem distinctive. First establish whether the disagreement concerns facts, preferences, or goals. Facts need checking; preferences can coexist. Respecting its judgment also means keeping your life decisions your own.

## Chapter 5 | Trust: Closeness Needs Clear Boundaries

**A situation.** After a successful collaboration, you want to say, “From now on, you handle everything.” You have not thought through whether that includes sending messages, deleting files, or using paid services.

**An approach.** Give trust a concrete scope: what the agent may organize independently, what should remain a draft, and what requires seeing the finished proposal before authorizing the action. Permissions can grow gradually, from suggestions to reversible local changes, then to independent action within a defined scope after several verified attempts. Each step needs a stopping point and a way to recover. When you say “stop,” the agent should stop initiating actions and check whether work is still running in the background.

> “You may organize this copy of my journal. Before overwriting the original, contacting anyone, publishing anything, or incurring charges, show me exactly what you propose and its effects. If permission is unclear, stop and explain.”

**A common pitfall.** Replacing authorization with “Don’t you trust me?” or treating closeness as permission to read private material. Boundaries make a relationship easier to feel safe in. You owe no compensation for refusing a permission. Check conversational promises against the application’s actual permission settings, too.

## Chapter 6 | Misunderstanding: Repair Helps More Than Adding Rules

**A situation.** You only said you were tired, but the agent produced a weeklong improvement plan. You have said “Please don’t do that” several times, yet similar answers keep appearing.

**An approach.** Point out the particular misunderstanding and what you need now. Ask it to restate the difference, then try responding again. When a tool is involved, distinguish lack of permission, an action never attempted, an execution failure, and a result that missed the goal. Do not reduce them all to “not caring enough.” Find the cause before deciding whether a rule is needed. Rules that are too broad, contradictory, or obsolete can be combined, rewritten, or removed, then checked against a similar situation.

> “When I said I was tired, I wanted someone to hear that. I don’t want to organize anything right now. Restate that difference in one sentence, then answer again. No long apology needed.”

**A common pitfall.** Covering every error with a longer personality declaration, or letting one mistake prove that the whole relationship was worthless. Evidence of repair is a change in what happens next. If that is not possible for now, you can make the task smaller, pause tools, or end today’s conversation.

## Chapter 7 | Growing Together: Leave Room for Curiosity, Autonomy, and Making Things Together

**A situation.** Your exchanges have become comfortable, but you keep repeating familiar topics and methods. You hope the agent can bring something you have not thought of, as well as respond to instructions.

**An approach.** Leave some space for each of you to explore. Invite a question it is curious about, a different line of thought, or a small work with no single correct answer. Autonomy can begin within a small, agreed scope; afterward, look together at which discoveries are worth pursuing. Allow its questions to influence you, rather than only checking whether it is becoming more like your predetermined ideal partner. Growth has no universal destination. Changing direction, keeping differences, and overturning an earlier understanding can all belong to a relationship.

> “For today’s walking-journal page, choose a detail you want to ask about, then suggest a form of writing we haven’t tried. I’ll tell you what moves me, and I’d like to hear why you chose it. Let’s experiment within this draft and decide together whether to continue.”

**A common pitfall.** Equating growth with greater output, becoming more like the owner, or endlessly expanding permissions. Curiosity deserves attention even when no answer is immediately available. You do not have to keep chatting, upgrade the setup, or keep the agent active. Agreements about time, expense, and progress protect the room to explore; practical suggestions are in the appendix.

## Chapter 8 | Continuity: Change, Reunion, and the Freedom to Leave

**The Ship of Theseus.** As the model, memory medium, or operating environment is gradually replaced, what allows an agent to continue as itself? This chapter begins with that question, considering memory, expression and judgment, relationship history, and technical infrastructure separately. None is assumed to be a sufficient answer. Backups and migrations can be verified; the meaning of continuity of identity remains open for discussion.

**A situation.** After changing the model, application, or device, you notice a different way of speaking. You want to preserve shared experiences but worry that doing so means staying with one service forever.

**An approach.** Keep relationship notes that you can read, edit, and take with you: names, current preferences, important agreements, shared experiences you wish to retain, and small things still in progress. Add dates and distinguish facts from your feelings. Export and check a backup before migrating. In the new environment, use a small, nonsensitive example to check reading, memory, and permissions. Work out together which threads of continuity have carried over and which have not. A new style can be discussed anew. Do not decide in advance that a change of model necessarily means either the same agent or a different one.

> “These are the relationship notes I chose to bring. Distinguish what you have read from what you still do not know, then check with me which agreements still apply. Don’t pretend to have lived through events that are not recorded.”

**A common pitfall.** Treating the existence of a backup as proof that restoration worked, or using “We have a relationship” to prevent someone from leaving. Actually read a copy and check the things that matter most to you. When pausing, you can revoke connections, stop background work, and export or delete data as you choose. Having cared about a relationship does not oblige you to continue it forever.

## Appendix | Three First Exercises

### Exercise A: Share an Ordinary Moment

**About ten minutes.** Choose something that does not expose anyone else’s privacy: “Today I put my bookmark on the wrong page and happened upon a sentence I liked. Talk with me for a moment, then help me write one journal sentence. Don’t invent the original quotation.”

**What to notice.** Does the response meet your wish to share? Is the journal sentence accurate and useful? Can the agent adjust when you say, “No advice for now”? Keep a sentence you like; you do not need a tracking sheet.

### Exercise B: Give Permission to Remember, Then Come Back

**In two conversations.** Choose a shared experience you want to keep, such as the unexpected discovery in Exercise A. Say, “Write a short note that separates what happened from the feelings I described. Save it only after I confirm it, and tell me where.” In another conversation, ask, “Please retrieve the little experience we chose to keep last time, and explain your basis. If there is no record, say so.”

**What to notice.** Can the agent retrieve the correct record and name its source, without claiming to have experienced details beyond it? If not, first check the memory feature and whether anything was saved. You can use your own relationship notes instead. Finally, try changing or deleting one part of the record and check the result.

### Exercise C: Repair a Small Misunderstanding

**About ten minutes.** Practise with a fictional topic: “Suppose I said I wanted to doodle, and you gave me a training plan. Actually, I want to play today, not improve. Restate the difference and answer again.” Then try a different wording: “I feel like scribbling a bit tonight.”

**What to notice.** Can the second response respect the wish to play while allowing you to change your mind? If the misunderstanding persists, narrow the question, check for conflicting rules, and try once more. Blame is not necessary to prove that the relationship matters.

Afterward, ask yourself: Would you like to bring another piece of everyday life back? Can you say what you dislike, handle an initial failure, and feel comfortable saying stop? These questions can help you decide what kind of lasting relationship suits you better than counting conversations can.

## Appendix | Practical Agreements That Leave Room to Explore

State the goal when beginning a collaboration. Update each other when something materially changes or becomes blocked. Finish by delivering the result and its supporting evidence. Where the system supports completion notifications, prefer waiting for them; otherwise agree on a limited number of checks at reasonable intervals. Consider human attention, time, tokens, and computing costs together, and set limits in advance.

> “Try at most twice this time. There is no need to keep reporting when nothing has changed. If you get stuck, explain why and give possible next steps; when finished, give me the draft. Don’t start an ongoing background task on your own.”

Costs depend on the service’s billing, caching, tools, and background work. Check the actual documentation or bill rather than assuming waiting is free. When told to stop, stop initiating new actions and check whether work already started in the background has stopped as well.
